How a starting balance and regular monthly deposits grow when the interest compounds each month.

The formula

Balance = start × (1 + r)n + deposit × ((1 + r)n − 1) ÷ r, where r is the yearly rate ÷ 12 and n is the number of months.

Deposits are assumed to go in at the end of each month and interest is added monthly, which is how most Australian savings accounts work.

Worked example

$5,000 to start, $200 a month, 4.5% a year, for 10 years. You put in $29,000 in total. The balance reaches about $38,075, so roughly $9,075 of it is interest. The interest earned in year 10 alone is about $1,615, nearly six times year 1's $280, because the balance is bigger and each year's interest earns interest too.

The catches with high-interest savings accounts

  • Bonus rate conditions. Many accounts only pay the headline rate if you deposit a set amount each month and make no withdrawals. Miss once and you get the much lower base rate for that month.
  • Introductory rates. A rate that drops after 4 months is not a 4-month bonus on a 10-year plan. Compare the ongoing rate.
  • Balance caps. Some bonus rates only apply up to a balance limit, such as $50,000 or $250,000.

Tax and inflation

Interest is taxable income, taxed at your marginal rate. On a 32% marginal rate a 4.5% account really earns about 3.1% after tax. If inflation runs at 3%, that is close to standing still in real terms. Savings accounts are for safety and short-term goals; the Government's Financial Claims Scheme guarantees deposits up to $250,000 per person per bank if an authorised bank fails.

For money you won't touch for many years, people often compare paying extra into a mortgage offset account (saving interest at the loan rate, with no tax on the saving) or extra super contributions. Which is right depends on your situation; Moneysmart, run by ASIC, has free guides on each.
