The formula

Litres = distance × L/100km ÷ 100. Cost = litres × price per litre.

Worked example: Melbourne to Sydney and back

The Hume Highway route is about 880 km each way. In a car using 7.5 L/100km, the return trip of 1,760 km burns 1,760 × 7.5 ÷ 100 = 132 litres. At $2.00 a litre that is $264, or $88 each for three people. Return flights for three people usually cost a good deal more, which is why driving often wins for groups (if you have the time).

Getting a realistic L/100km figure

  • The official figure on your car's windscreen sticker comes from a lab test. Real-world use is commonly 10-20% higher.
  • Highway driving at a steady 100-110 km/h is usually close to the sticker's "extra-urban" number. City driving is much worse.
  • A loaded roof rack, a trailer or a caravan can add 20-50% or more. If you tow, use your trip computer's figure from a previous towing trip.
  • The most accurate method: fill the tank, reset the trip meter, drive, refill, then divide litres added by kilometres driven and multiply by 100.

Paying less for fuel

Petrol prices in the capital cities move in a regular cycle, often swinging 30-40 cents a litre over a few weeks. Filling up at the bottom of the cycle saves more than most other tricks. Most states have free price-checking apps (for example NSW FuelCheck and WA FuelWatch), and the ACCC publishes where each city sits in its cycle. On a long trip, country prices are often higher than city prices, so fill up before you leave town.

Splitting costs fairly

Fuel is only part of what a car trip costs the owner. Tyres, servicing and depreciation add up too, which is why the ATO's cents-per-kilometre rate for work travel (88 cents per km for 2024-25) is far above the cost of fuel alone. If one person is lending the car for a long trip, offering a little more than the fuel share is a fair gesture.
