Appliance running cost calculator

What an appliance costs to run, from its wattage, how long it runs and your electricity price.

{{ad}}

The formula

kWh = watts × hours ÷ 1,000. Cost = kWh × cents per kWh ÷ 100.

A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is what your bill charges for: 1,000 watts running for one hour. Your bill also has a daily supply charge that you pay no matter what you switch on; it isn't included here.

Worked example: heating a room in winter

A 2,400 W fan heater running 4 hours a night uses 9.6 kWh. At 33c per kWh that is $3.17 a night, about $289 over a three-month winter.

A reverse-cycle split system heating the same room typically averages around 800 W, because it moves heat in from outside rather than making it. That's 3.2 kWh a night, or $1.06, about $96 a quarter. The same warmth for a third of the cost is why energy agencies push heat pumps.

Finding the wattage

  • Look for the rating plate or sticker on the back or base of the appliance, or in the manual. It shows the maximum, in watts (W) or kilowatts (kW).
  • Appliances that cycle on and off, like fridges, heaters with thermostats and air conditioners, use less than the maximum on average. For those, the yearly kWh on the Energy Rating label is a better guide: multiply it by your price per kWh to get the yearly cost directly.
  • A plug-in power meter (around $20-30 at hardware stores) shows the real draw over a day and is the most accurate way to check.

What price per kWh to use

Check your latest bill. Most Australian households pay somewhere between about 25 and 45 cents per kWh depending on state, retailer and plan. If you are on a time-of-use plan, use the rate for when the appliance actually runs: running a dishwasher or pool pump in the off-peak window, or in the middle of the day if you have solar panels, can cut its cost by half or more.

Standby power

Devices left on standby still draw a few watts each. Ten devices at 3 W is 30 W all day: 0.72 kWh, about 24c a day at 33c per kWh, or close to $87 a year for nothing. A switch-off-at-the-wall habit or a standby-saving power board pays for itself fast.
